§ 1 · Parse

A PDF is a drawing, not a document.

A PDF only knows where to paint each letter. The parser reads the layout instead: headings from font size and weight, tables kept as tables. Every block keeps its exact character offsets, so any answer can point back to its place on the page.

why not plain text?Plain text loses the headings, and headings are where a contract's structure lives. 100 of 100 contracts parsed; 0 offset errors in the check.
§ 2 · Chunk (i)

Cut it into windows.

Search works on pieces, so the contract is cut. Fixed windows of 512 tokens take no notice of meaning: the scissors land mid-sentence. A 64-token overlap is what saves this clause, which sits exactly where two windows overlap.

why so crude?Because it won. Of three strategies, fixed windows put the evidence in the top 8 most often: 0.85 of the time, against 0.74 and 0.58.
§ 2 · Chunk (ii)

…or by section…

Section-aware chunking follows the contract's own headings: "4. Term and Termination" becomes one piece. It is the most lawyer-like cut, and its answers are the most faithful to their sources.

so why not this?Long sections dilute the match and short ones lose context, so retrieval found the evidence less often: recall@8 0.74 against 0.85.
§ 2 · Chunk (iii)

…or sentence by sentence.

The sharpest cut: one sentence is searched, and the model is shown its neighbours too. Here the whole clause is exactly one sentence.

the catchA single sentence rarely shares enough words with a question: recall@8 0.58. The model still read more than recall suggests, 0.71–0.75 of the evidence, thanks to the neighbours.
§ 3 · The question

First, strike the contract's name.

Every question names its contract. Once the search is limited to that contract, the name only matches the title page and the signature blocks, and they win first place. So the name is struck out before searching.

one line of codeThe single biggest improvement in the project: mean reciprocal rank 0.34 → 0.73 for keyword search.
§ 4 · Search

Keywords against meaning.

Two ways to rank the contract's 12 chunks: BM25, which counts shared words, and an embedding model, which compares meaning. On this question, meaning search put the clause 1st and keywords put it 5th.

and still BM25?Across the 59 test questions, keywords ranked the evidence higher: MRR 0.73 against 0.47–0.50. Combining the two tied BM25 at extra cost, and a reranker hurt (0.56) while adding a second per question. Fifth place still makes the top 8, which is why the model reads eight excerpts, not one.
§ 5 · Answer

Cite it, or say it isn't there.

The top eight chunks are numbered [1] to [8] and handed to qwen3.5:4b, which runs on the laptop. Its instructions: answer only from the excerpts, cite each claim, and if they don't cover the question, say so.

why so strict?A citation makes every claim checkable. When the contract really doesn't cover a question, it declines 91% of the time instead of guessing.
§ 6 · Check

Check every sentence, not just the answer.

A second, larger model from another family, gemma4:12b, splits the answer into statements and checks each one against only the excerpts it cites. Plain code also checks that every number in a statement appears in its excerpt.

why a different model?Models rate their own style higher. The grader was itself checked against reference labels, and one rule that looked stricter was rejected because it agreed worse (κ 0.18). The adopted one agrees at κ 0.60.
§ 7 · Diagnose

When it fails, find the stage that broke.

Every answer is traced through the saved rankings in order: was the evidence in a chunk, ranked, delivered, whole, answered, supported? This one passes every check. Of 560 answers, 185 didn't.

what it showed62% of failures happen before the model sees the evidence, mostly two-part questions where only one half's clause is found. That's the next thing to fix, not the model.
