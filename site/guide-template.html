Field guide · every part of the project, explained

How contract-rag reads a contract, part by part.

contract-rag answers questions about real commercial contracts and cites the clause each answer came from. This guide walks through every file in the repository in the order data flows through it. For each part you get what it does, a small animation of it working on real data, the files that hold it, and why it was built that way instead of another way.

    Part one · built once, offline

    Building the library

    Article I

    The contracts: CUAD, and a 100-contract working set

    Everything starts from a public dataset of real agreements that lawyers have already marked up, so every answer can be checked against a human reading.

    CUAD (the Contract Understanding Atticus Dataset) is 510 commercial contracts filed publicly with the SEC. Lawyers highlighted the exact text for 41 kinds of clause, such as governing law, termination for convenience, cap on liability and non-compete, over 13,000 highlights in all. Those highlights become the project's answer key.

    The download step fetches the archive from Zenodo and checks its MD5 fingerprint. Downloads resume after a dropped connection, and the file is only renamed into place once complete. Extraction is safe to re-run. It then reconciles three inventories that don't line up on their own: the PDFs, the plain-text copies, and the lawyers' spreadsheet (master_clauses.csv). The result is one manifest row per contract.

    Every experiment runs on 100 of the 510. They are picked by stratified sampling, so each of the 25 contract types keeps its share, and each type gets at least one. A fixed seed (42) means the same 100 come back every time. Three tiny contracts are also copied into tests/fixtures/, so the test suite runs in seconds without the real data.

    Files
    ingest/download.pyResumable, checksum-verified download; safe extraction; the manifest (build_manifest); the stratified subset (stratified_subset); the 3-contract test fixture (build_fixture).
    configs/ingest.yamlArchive URL and MD5, subset size (100), seed (42).
    schemas.pyThe data shapes every stage passes on: Document, Block, Chunk, EvalQuestion, RetrievalResult, GenerationResult, EvalScores.
    why this, not that

    CUAD over synthetic or scraped contracts: it's real, messy legal text with answers a lawyer marked. That makes "is the answer right?" a measurement rather than an opinion.

    100 contracts, not 510: every design choice is an experiment re-run many times on one laptop. 100 keeps a full search comparison to about 16 minutes.

    Article II

    Reading the PDFs, not just their text

    The parser keeps what plain text throws away: which lines are headings, which are tables, what page they're on, and exactly where each piece sits in the document.

    The parser uses PyMuPDF to read every text block on a page, along with its font size and how much of it is bold. Each block is then sorted into one of five kinds:

    • Heading: it starts with a clause number (4., 12.2, Section 3, Article IV), or it looks like one. That means it's short, it isn't a full sentence, and it's bold (45% or more), or at least 1.15× the body font size, or all capitals.
    • Table: found by page.find_tables() and kept as a Markdown table, so rows and columns survive.
    • Paragraph, list item or other for the rest.

    Running headers and footers are dropped. Short text in the top or bottom margin that repeats on at least half the pages ("Page 3 of 12", "CONFIDENTIAL") would otherwise land in the middle of clauses.

    Lines are joined, words split by a hyphen at a line break are rejoined, and the text is normalized. The result is one Document.full_text per contract. Every block records its character offsets into that text, its page, and its section path (for example ["4. Term and Termination"]). Everything downstream points back into the document through those offsets.

    Each document's parse is cached on disk, and a crash only loses the file in progress.

    Files
    ingest/parse_pdf.pyparse_pdf for one file; _repeated_margin_keys (headers/footers); _numbered_heading and _looks_like_visual_heading; table_to_markdown; parse_manifest with per-document caching.
    ingest/normalize.pyUnicode and whitespace normalization; joins wrapped lines and repairs hyphenation.
    configs/parse_pdf.yamlInput manifest, output paths, margin and heading thresholds.
    why this, not that

    Our own parse, not CUAD's .txt files: the text files have no headings, tables or pages, and section-aware chunking and page citations need all three.

    PyMuPDF over heavier layout tools: it's fast, runs locally, and exposes fonts and bold directly. That was enough to reach 0 parse failures.

    Catch: CUAD's highlight offsets point into its text files, not ours. Article VII shows how the answer key is re-aligned.

    Article III

    Cutting the text into chunks, three different ways

    Search returns pieces, not whole contracts. Where the cuts fall decides whether a clause arrives whole, split in two, or buried in unrelated text, so the cut is an experiment variable.

    All three strategies cut on exact character offsets, count tokens with tiktoken (cl100k_base), and give each chunk a stable id, {doc}::{strategy}::{index}, which the answer later cites.

    • Fixed: windows of 512 tokens that overlap by 64, cut wherever the count runs out. 2,166 chunks; nearly all are exactly 512 tokens.
    • Section: a new chunk at every detected heading; a section longer than 512 tokens is subdivided, and nothing is ever merged across headings. 5,578 chunks, median 110 tokens.
    • Sentence window: one sentence per chunk (NLTK's Punkt splitter, taught legal abbreviations so "Sec." or "Inc." don't end a sentence). The model is shown that sentence with three sentences either side (context_text). 20,898 chunks, median 34 tokens.

    Each chunk also keeps its section path, pages, and whether it holds a table, so an answer can say where it read something.

    What the figure shows

    The same passage of the Kubient × Associated Press services agreement, cut all three ways. The underlined sentence is the clause a lawyer highlighted for "can a party end it early without cause?".

    Files
    chunking/base.pyBaseChunker: exact offsets, token counting, token windows, the deepest shared section path.
    chunking/fixed.pyFixedChunker: 512-token windows with a 64-token overlap.
    chunking/section.pySectionChunker: splits at headings, subdivides long sections, never merges across one.
    chunking/sentence_window.pySentenceWindowChunker: Punkt sentences with legal abbreviations, ±3-sentence context.
    chunking/run.pyRuns the strategies from configs/chunking.yaml; writes the chunk files and chunk_stats.json.
    why this, not that

    Three strategies instead of one: nobody knows the right cut for contracts in advance, so it was measured. Fixed won search (recall@8 0.85, against 0.74 for section and 0.58 for sentence). Most contracts are only about 13 fixed chunks long, so the right one is usually close to the top.

    But smaller chunks give more faithful answers (Article X). A 512-token chunk holds several provisions, and a small model sometimes mixes them up.

    Article IV

    Two kinds of index: words and meanings

    Every chunk is indexed twice: once by the words it contains (BM25) and once by what it means (embeddings), so the two can be compared, and combined.

    BM25: matching words

    BM25 is the classic keyword ranking. A chunk scores higher the more of the question's words it contains, especially rare words, with a correction so long chunks don't win just by being long (k1 = 1.5, b = 0.75). Before counting, common words like "a", "the" and "and" are dropped and the rest are stemmed, so "notice", "notices" and "noticed" all become notic. The index is built with bm25s, and it scores every chunk for every query, so limiting a search to one contract is exact rather than a filter over a cut-off list.

    Embeddings: matching meaning

    An embedding model turns a text into a list of numbers such that texts with similar meaning land near each other, so "end the agreement early" can find "terminate for convenience" with no shared word. Three open models run locally: bge-small (33M parameters), bge-base (110M) and e5-base (110M). small vs base tests size, and bge vs e5 tests model family. The vectors live in a local Qdrant database, one collection per chunking × model, with the contract id indexed so a search can stay inside one contract.

    Embedding 28,642 chunks three times is the slowest part of the build, so every vector is cached in SQLite, keyed by the model name and a SHA-256 hash of the exact text. Fixing a bug elsewhere never forces anything to be re-embedded.

    Files
    retrieval/bm25.pyBM25Index (stemming, stopwords, save/load) and BM25Retriever, which returns only chunks sharing a term with the query.
    retrieval/embed.pySentenceTransformerEncoder (Apple-GPU when available) and EmbeddingCache in SQLite; embed_texts encodes only what's missing.
    retrieval/dense.pybuild_collection (cosine distance, doc_id payload index) and DenseRetriever.
    retrieval/build.pyBuilds every index for every strategy and model, then runs smoke queries.
    configs/retrieval.yamlBM25 parameters, the three embedding models and their query prefixes, the two rerankers.
    why this, not that

    Keep BM25 even though embeddings are "smarter": contracts repeat their own defined terms exactly ("Initial Term", "written notice"). Measured on the test questions, embeddings alone ranked the first relevant chunk 0.24 lower in MRR than BM25 (Article VIII).

    Local Qdrant, not a hosted vector database: no account, no network and no cost, and it's the same API as the hosted version if the project ever scales.

    Part two · on every question

    Answering a question

    Article V

    Searching: scope, fuse, rerank

    One pipeline turns a question into the 8 excerpts the model will read. Every component can be switched on or off, and every intermediate ranking is saved.

    1. Scope to one contract. Questions are about a named contract, so search runs inside it. The contract's name is then replaced by "the agreement". Inside one contract the name only matches the title page, preamble and signature block, which would otherwise outrank the actual clause. This one line (scoped_query) raised MRR by 0.40, the biggest single gain in the project.
    2. Gather candidates. BM25 and/or one embedding model each return their top 50.
    3. Fuse when both are on. Their scores live on different scales, so they can't simply be added. Reciprocal Rank Fusion ignores scores and adds 1 / (60 + rank) from each list. Weighted fusion rescales each list to 0–1 and mixes them with a weight α, which was tuned on the dev questions only (usually 0.4 on the embeddings).
    4. Rerank (optional). A cross-encoder reads the question and each candidate together, which is more precise than comparing two separately computed vectors but far slower, so it only sees the shortlist. Two were tried: bge-reranker-base (278M) and MiniLM (22M).
    5. Cut to the top 8.

    The result keeps every stage's ranked list and its latency (RetrievalResult.stages). That record is what lets failure analysis say "BM25 had it at rank 3; the reranker pushed it to 14".

    Files
    retrieval/pipeline.pyscoped_query; RetrievalPipeline (candidates → fusion → rerank → top k, every stage recorded); RetrievalResources loads indexes and models lazily and shares them.
    retrieval/fusion.pyreciprocal_rank_fusion and weighted_fusion (min-max scaled).
    retrieval/rerank.pyCrossEncoderReranker over the candidates.
    retrieval/config.pyIndex and model settings; one Qdrant collection name per (chunking, model).
    why this, not that

    The served setup has no fusion and no reranker. On the test questions, adding embeddings to BM25 changed MRR by −0.02 (not significant). The bge reranker lowered it by 0.15 and costs about 1 s per question. Inside one contract (a median of 13 fixed chunks), plain BM25 already has the answer on its shortlist.

    Everything kept switchable anyway: on a larger corpus, or with questions across contracts, the balance could flip, and the switches let it be re-measured with one config change.

    Article VI

    Answering, with a citation on every sentence

    A small open model, running on the laptop through Ollama, answers only from the numbered excerpts and has to say which excerpt each sentence came from, or that the contract doesn't say.

    The 8 excerpts are numbered [1]…[8] and labelled with their section and pages. The instructions are strict:

    1. Use only the excerpts; no outside knowledge.
    2. End every sentence with the excerpt number(s) behind it, like [2] or [1][3].
    3. Use the contract's exact wording for dates, amounts, notice periods and governing law.
    4. If the excerpts don't contain the answer, set "abstained": true and don't guess.
    5. Answer only what was asked, in at most 4 sentences.

    The reply must be JSON ({"answer": …, "abstained": …}), which Ollama enforces with a schema. Temperature is 0 and the seed fixed, so reruns give the same answer. Hidden "thinking" is turned off. The context window is raised to 8,192 tokens because the default is too small for 8 excerpts.

    Afterwards, parse_citations maps each [n] back to a real chunk id and counts any number that points at no excerpt (a [9] when there were 8). Token counts and latency are logged on every call.

    Files
    generation/prompts.pySYSTEM_PROMPT, format_context (numbered excerpts with section and pages), build_messages, ANSWER_SCHEMA, parse_citations.
    generation/generator.pyGeneratorConfig (model, temperature 0, 8k context, 400-token limit, thinking off); OllamaGenerator; parse_output with a fallback for invalid JSON.
    why this, not that

    [1]…[8] instead of chunk ids: small models copy a short number far more reliably than an id like cuad_0042::section::00031.

    qwen3.5:4b: open weights, small enough for a 24 GB laptop, and a different family from the grading model (Article IX), so the grader isn't marking its own family's work. The 2B size was also measured: it left the citations out of 52 of 70 answers.

    Part three · the evaluation harness

    Measuring it

    Article VII

    The exam: 100 questions and an answer key

    Before any model is judged, there has to be a fixed set of questions with known answers, and a precise rule for which chunks count as "the evidence".

    The questions

    100 questions are generated from the lawyers' highlights and the spreadsheet's reference answers, each about one named contract. There are four kinds:

    • 60 single-clause questions ("Which state's law governs…?"),
    • 15 multi-part questions whose evidence sits in two far-apart places,
    • 10 numeric questions (amounts, caps, durations),
    • 15 unanswerable questions about a clause the contract doesn't have. These test whether the model admits it.

    Categories are drawn round-robin, so no clause type dominates, with at most 2 questions per contract. Contracts, not questions, are split into 30 dev / 70 test, so no contract appears on both sides. Every setting is tuned on dev, and every reported number comes from test. All 100 were read by hand, and 6 whose highlight didn't actually support the question were swapped out, with the reasons recorded in configs/eval.yaml.

    The answer key

    A lawyer's highlight is a piece of text, and its offsets point into CUAD's own text file. The labeller finds the same text in our parse. First it tries an exact match after normalizing (lowercase, letters and digits only). If that fails it falls back to a fuzzy match (rapidfuzz, score ≥ 85). 100% of highlights were located for all three chunkings.

    A chunk is relevant if it overlaps the highlight by at least half of whichever of the two is smaller. That way a short sentence inside a long chunk counts, and so does a long highlight spread over several short chunks.

    Files
    eval/build_questions.pyCandidates per question type, the round-robin select, the contract-level split_documents, reviewed replacements, and a review sheet.
    eval/labels.pyDocText (normalized text with a map back to offsets), exact-then-fuzzy alignment, is_relevant (the 50%-of-the-smaller rule), alignment_report.
    data/eval/questions.jsonlThe committed exam: question, contract, type, category, reference answer, evidence, split.
    why this, not that

    A committed exam, not questions made up on the fly: every setup is compared on exactly the same 100, so a difference comes from the setup and not from the questions.

    Split by contract: tuning on one clause of a contract and testing on another clause of the same contract would leak information and flatter the results.

    Article VIII

    Measuring search: 189 setups, picked on dev, reported on test

    Search can be scored without any language model, so the whole grid of combinations runs and every component has to earn its place with a number.

    The scores

    • Evidence recall@k: the share of highlighted pieces of evidence with a relevant chunk in the top k. It's the headline number at k = 8, which is what the model receives. The model needs each piece of evidence once, however many chunks it was split into.
    • MRR: one over the rank of the first relevant chunk (1 if it's first, ½ if second…).
    • nDCG@10 and context precision, which reward relevant chunks near the top.

    Every mean comes with a 95% bootstrap interval: the questions are resampled 2,000 times, and the mean is recomputed each time. Two setups are compared question by question (a paired difference). A difference is only called real when its interval excludes zero.

    The grid

    3 chunkings × {BM25, 3 embedding models, both fused by RRF or weighted} × {no reranker, 2 rerankers} × {inside the contract, across all 100}, plus variants that keep the contract name in the query. That's 189 setups, scored in 16 minutes. Identical searches and reranker scores are computed once and reused, and latency is only timed on uncached calls. Setups that tie on every dev question are folded into one, so a tie can't take two of the slots.

    Files
    eval/retrieval_metrics.pyThe only place recall is computed: recall@k, MRR, nDCG, context precision, bootstrap_ci, paired_difference.
    eval/ablation.pyBuilds the grid, CachedRetriever and CachingScorer, per-component latency, dev selection with tie folding, the report.
    docs/ablations.mdThe full result tables.
    why this, not that

    Pick on dev, report on test: choosing the best of 189 on the same questions it's scored on would overstate it. Picking on 26 dev questions and reporting on 59 unseen ones keeps the number honest.

    Intervals on everything: with 59 questions, a 0.03 gap is often noise. Most "improvements" here turned out not to be real.

    Article IX

    Grading answers, one statement at a time

    A second, larger open model grades every answer. It never sees the whole context at once: each statement is checked only against the excerpts that statement cites.

    The grader is gemma4:12b, from a different model family than the one answering, so it isn't grading its own family's writing. Its work is split so each step is small and checkable:

    1. Split in code. The answer is cut into statements, each one taking the citation numbers that follow it. "A within 30 days [3], B within a reasonable time [4]" becomes two statements.
    2. Check each statement against only its own excerpts. The grader answers two questions: is every fact stated in, or directly implied by, those excerpts? Is it on topic? A statement can't be "supported" by an excerpt it didn't cite.
    3. Check numbers in code. Every figure in a statement (days, amounts, dates, section numbers) has to appear in its cited excerpts, as digits or as words. A number that isn't there makes the statement unsupported.
    4. Grade correctness in a separate call, against the lawyer-derived reference and the highlighted clause: correct = 1, partial = ½, incorrect = 0.

    From these come faithfulness (the share of statements supported), citation validity (the share of citation numbers that point at a real excerpt), relevance (the share on topic), correctness, and, for the unanswerable questions, abstention accuracy: did it decline, by its flag or by saying so in its first sentence?

    Can the grader be trusted?

    Only after it was checked. 30 dev questions were answered from a controlled context (the evidence blocks plus 3 distractors, so search is out of the loop). The grader's verdicts were then compared with 24 reference labels written blind. Agreement is reported as Cohen's κ, which discounts the agreement two raters would reach by chance. The correctness rubric went through versions. The one in use, contradiction-only v5, reached κ 0.60 (83% agreement). A "list the key facts" rubric that looked more rigorous only reached κ 0.18, and was rejected.

    Files
    eval/judge.pysplit_statements, OllamaJudge (statement check + grade), unsupported_numbers, statement_check, faithfulness_score, citation_validity, declines, score_answer; the rubric is RUBRIC.
    eval/calibration.pyControlled-context answers (oracle_chunks) and a blind labelling sheet with no grader output on it.
    eval/agreement.pycohen_kappa, percent agreement, and the mapping from scores to labels.
    why this, not that

    Statement by statement, not "rate this answer 1–10": a single score hides which sentence is wrong. Per-statement checks against the cited excerpt catch a right fact with the wrong citation, as in the example.

    Numbers checked in code: small graders let a changed figure slide ("60 days" for "90 days"). A string check doesn't.

    Report the agreement: a 12B grader is noisy, so its κ is published next to every score. The scores are best used to compare setups, not as absolute truth.

    Article X

    The full run: 8 setups × 70 questions = 560 graded answers

    The best search setups from Article VIII are run end to end, search → answer → grade, on every test question, on a 24 GB laptop.

    The run takes the top 4 dev picks, the best setup for each chunking, and a BM25-only baseline for each chunking: 8 setups in all. A laptop can't hold the 4B answering model and the 12B grader at once, so the run goes in three phases, with one model in memory per phase:

    1. Search for all 8 setups × 70 questions.
    2. Answer with qwen3.5:4b. When two setups hand the model the same excerpts for a question, the answer is reused rather than regenerated.
    3. Grade with gemma4:12b. Identical answer-plus-excerpts pairs are graded once.

    Every result is appended to disk as it's produced, so a crash resumes where it stopped. The model server is told to unload every 10 calls, because Ollama's memory grows by gigabytes over hundreds of requests. A re-grade mode copies an earlier run's searches and answers, reuses its statement verdicts, and only regrades correctness. That's how the fixed grader was applied to the same 560 answers without changing any of them.

    What came out: correctness ranges from 0.65 to 0.75, and the plain BM25 setup is at the top. Nothing beat it on correctness by a significant margin. Smaller chunks (section, sentence window) give far more faithful answers, 0.83–0.87 against 0.62, because each excerpt holds one provision instead of several.

    Files
    eval/run_matrix.pychoose_configs / configs_to_run, the three phases in run, answer and verdict reuse, _unload, the report with paired differences against the baseline.
    configs/matrix.yamlWhich setups, which models, bootstrap size, how often to unload.
    docs/answer_quality.mdThe final table.
    why this, not that

    Only 8 setups get answers: search scores are free, but answering and grading cost about 30 s per question on a laptop. The expensive stage only runs on setups that already won the cheap one, plus baselines to compare against.

    Reuse, don't recompute: many setups hand the model identical excerpts. Reusing those answers made the run affordable and makes the comparison fairer, because the same input gets the same answer.

    Article XI

    Why answers fail, and where

    Every answer that wasn't fully correct is traced back through the saved pipeline data to the first stage that went wrong. 20 of them were also read by hand.

    Because every stage's output was saved, each failure can be walked through in order. The first check that fails names it:

    1. Is the evidence inside some chunk at all? If not: lost in chunking.
    2. Did BM25 or the embeddings rank a chunk that holds it? If not: retrieval miss.
    3. Did any of it reach the model? If not: ranking miss, or reranker demotion if the reranker pushed it out.
    4. Did all of it reach the model? If not: partial evidence.
    5. Did the model answer? If not: refused with evidence.
    6. Is every statement supported? If not: unsupported claims; if they are all supported but the answer is still wrong: misread evidence.

    Unanswerable questions the model answered anyway are answered unanswerable.

    The hand review found that the first grader was part of the problem. 58% of its non-correct grades faulted details "not in the clause" that were true elsewhere in the contract. Its abstention score mostly read a JSON flag, while the answer text said "the contract doesn't say". Fixing both and regrading the same answers cut failures from 282 to 185 and raised mean correctness from 0.59 to 0.71, without changing a single answer.

    With grading fixed, 62% of the remaining failures happen before the model sees the evidence. The main causes: multi-part questions, where one half dominates the search, and vocabulary gaps ("restrict from competing" against "shall not enter into an agreement with Futurestep").

    Files
    analysis/failures.pytrace_spans (where each piece of evidence got to), categorize, triage_row, checks (tests the hand-review patterns on all 560 rows), the report.
    docs/failure_taxonomy.mdThe taxonomy, 20 hand-read cases, root causes and fixes.
    data/eval/failure_review.jsonlThe hand labels.
    why this, not that

    Trace, don't guess: "the model is dumb" and "search missed it" need opposite fixes. Saving every stage's ranking made the answer a lookup instead of an argument.

    Doubt the grader too: the biggest single improvement in this project changed no model and no search. It fixed how answers were scored.

    Part four · online

    Serving it

    Article XII

    The service: an API, a page, and one container

    The winning setup from the experiments is wrapped in a small web service with a key, logs and timings, packed into a Docker image and run on a free Hugging Face Space.

    The API

    QAService loads the parsed contracts, their fixed chunks and the BM25 index once at start-up. For each question it runs the served pipeline (fixed chunks, BM25, inside the chosen contract, top 8), asks the model, and returns the answer with every citation expanded: page, section and the excerpt's text. A lock makes sure only one question uses the model at a time.

    • GET /health is open; GET /contracts and POST /ask need an X-API-Key header, compared in constant time.
    • Every response carries an X-Request-ID, and every request writes one JSON log line: status, time, contract, whether the model declined, citations, tokens, and the search/answer split.
    • Errors say what happened: 404 for an unknown contract, 422 for a question that's too long, 502 when the model server fails.
    • The server refuses to start without a key unless explicitly run with --no-auth.

    A small Streamlit page lets anyone pick a contract, ask, and open each citation.

    The container

    The image installs only what the API imports: no PyTorch, because the served setup never embeds. It bakes in the 13 MB of data it needs, runs as an unprivileged user, and has a health check. On the Space, the same image also carries Ollama with qwen3.5:4b, with nginx in front: / is the page and /api/ is the API.

    Choosing the model for a free CPU

    The free Space has 2 CPU cores and no GPU. On 2 cores the 4B model takes about 2½ minutes per answer, almost all of it reading the ~4,500-token prompt. The smaller Qwen sizes are faster, so they were measured on the same 70 test questions with the same grader. They answer faster but mostly stop citing, and citations are the point of the project. So the Space runs the 4B that every result on this page was measured with, and asks for patience.

    Files
    api/service.pyQAService, AskResponse and Citation, display_title (turns CUAD file names into readable titles).
    api/app.pycreate_app: routes, the key check, request ids, one JSON log line per request, error mapping.
    api/main.pyStarts uvicorn; refuses to run without APP_API_KEY; --root-path for a proxy prefix.
    ui/streamlit_app.pyThe page: contract picker, question box, expandable citations, timings.
    DockerfileThe API image (610 MB); data staged by scripts/stage_image.py.
    deploy/space/The Space image (Ollama + model + API + page + nginx), assembled and uploaded by scripts/push_space.py.
    why this, not that

    A Hugging Face Space: the free tier has 16 GB of memory, enough for a 4B model on CPU. The other free hosts are too small to hold a model at all.

    The slow 4B, not a fast 2B: measured on the same test, the 2B's correctness fell from 0.75 to 0.53 and its citation validity from 0.87 to 0.14. A fast answer without its source isn't what this project promises.

    No paid API anywhere: every model in the project is open-weight and runs locally, which was a rule from the first day.

    Appendix A

    Run it yourself

    Every number on this page can be rebuilt from a clean checkout with these commands, in this order. Each one reads its settings from configs/ and writes to data/, indexes/ or runs/.

    # once: Python 3.11, the package, and the two local models
make setup
ollama pull qwen3.5:4b && ollama pull gemma4:12b
export PYTHONPATH=src

# build the library (Articles I–IV)
.venv/bin/python scripts/download_cuad.py      # CUAD → manifests, 100-contract subset, test fixture
.venv/bin/python scripts/parse_cuad.py         # PDFs → documents.jsonl + blocks.jsonl
.venv/bin/python scripts/chunk_cuad.py         # blocks → three chunk files
.venv/bin/python scripts/build_indexes.py      # BM25 + vectors (embeddings cached)

# measure (Articles VII–XI)
.venv/bin/python scripts/build_questions.py    # the 100-question exam

    Appendix C

    Glossary

    RAG (retrieval-augmented generation)
    Find the relevant passages first, then have a language model answer from them, instead of from memory.
    Chunk
    A piece of a contract that search can return. Its id is what an answer cites.
    BM25
    Keyword ranking: rare shared words count most, long chunks get no free advantage.
    Stemming
    Cutting words to a common root ("notice", "noticed" → notic) so they match.
    Embedding / dense retrieval
    Text turned into a vector so that similar meanings are close together; search by nearest vectors.
    Qdrant
    The vector database, run locally from a folder.
    RRF (reciprocal rank fusion)
    Merge rankings by adding 1 / (60 + rank) from each; ignores raw scores.
    Cross-encoder reranker
    A model that reads question and passage together to rescore a shortlist. More precise, much slower.
    Recall@k (evidence recall)
    Share of the highlighted evidence that has a relevant chunk in the top k results.
    MRR
    Mean of 1 / rank of the first relevant chunk. 1.0 means it's always first.
    nDCG@10
    A score for the whole top 10 that rewards relevant chunks nearer the top.
    Dev / test split
    Tune on dev, report on test, never the other way round, so results aren't flattered.
    Bootstrap interval
    Resample the questions 2,000 times to see how much a mean could move; the 95% range is reported.
    Paired difference
    Compare two setups question by question; "significant" means the interval excludes zero.
    Abstention
    The model saying the contract doesn't answer the question, instead of guessing.
    Faithfulness
    Share of an answer's statements supported by the excerpts they cite.
    Citation validity
    Share of citation numbers that point at an excerpt that was actually provided.
    Correctness
    Agreement with the lawyer-derived reference: correct 1, partial ½, incorrect 0.
    LLM-as-judge
    Using a language model to grade another's answers; trusted only after checking it against labels.
    Cohen's κ
    Agreement between two raters beyond what chance would give. 0.6 is "moderate to substantial".
    Ollama
    Local server that runs open-weight models on the laptop (or a CPU container).
    Ablation
    Switching components on and off one at a time to measure what each contributes.
